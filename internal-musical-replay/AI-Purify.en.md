## Stage 0 — Starting Point: Internal Musical Replay and R → R\* Drift

**Core of your understanding**  
After listening to music, the human mind often continues to replay it involuntarily (involuntary musical imagery / earworms). The representation R formed during initial listening undergoes subtle deformation, emphasis, and omission through repeated internal replay, and accumulates as a drifted representation R\*.

**Cross-check with primary / major sources**  
INMI / earworms as a phenomenon are firmly established. Liikkanen & Jakubowski (2020) in *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* review 47 studies from 33 articles. Empirical findings on frequency, duration, content, triggers, and individual differences are substantial.

**Current academic consensus and related literature**  
That INMI is a common and widespread psychological phenomenon is consensus. However, research that directly measures how representation drifts through internal replay (R → R\*) is almost absent. Jakubowski and colleagues have shown relatively high accuracy of tempo in spontaneous and voluntary musical imagery, but this indicates limited drift on certain structural dimensions and does not rule out affective or semantic drift.

**Confirmed nuances and points of contention**  
The direction that “R is not preserved unchanged” is consistent with general findings on involuntary memory (reconstruction and reinterpretation occur). Whether music-specific drift large enough to change emotion scores occurs remains unresolved.

**Present status and open issues**  
The INMI phenomenon itself is strongly supported. Formalizing the process as R → R\* drift, and the question of how it modulates affective responses on re-listening, currently stand as original hypotheses.

## Stage 1 — Distinguishing Knowledge Learning from Music Learning

**Core of your understanding**  
Knowledge learning is fundamentally about correction against a ground truth. Music has no such ground truth. What occurs are mismatches with experience (weaker uplift than expected, noticing disliked parts, changed understanding of lyrics). In this case, the observable outcome of learning should be treated as affective fluctuation, not correctness.

**Cross-check with primary / major sources**  
No primary text directly states this distinction. However, research on the functional uses of music (Saarikallio, Juslin & Sloboda, and others) typically describes the outcomes of musical experience in terms of emotion regulation, mood management, and changes in self-definition. Learning framed as “approach to a correct answer” is common in the machine-learning framing of MER, but is not the mainstream framework for explaining everyday human musical learning.

**Current academic consensus and related literature**  
Distinguishing error-driven / supervised learning from affective / aesthetic learning is standard in cognitive and affective science. Formalizing that, for music, the observable outcome of learning is affective fluctuation is an original formulation, but is consistent with the direction of everyday music-use research.

**Confirmed nuances and points of contention**  
Music learning does involve certain normative standards (correct performance, correct harmony) in technical and theoretical domains. The distinction you draw applies to the affective–experiential layer, where no ground truth exists; that restriction is valid.

**Present status and open issues**  
The distinction is consistent with related fields and stands as an original formalization. Whether treating affective fluctuation as the observable outcome is more appropriate than error-correction remains a question for future verification.

## Stage 2 — The Proposal of Machine Internal Rehearsal (“Dream Training”)

**Core of your understanding**  
Deliberately induce the human INMI process inside a machine. Subject an initially learned R to repeated internal rehearsal, generate and accumulate drifted R\*, and observe how emotion predictions change when R\* is re-entered.

**Cross-check with primary / major sources**  
Existing MER research is largely one-shot mapping from acoustic features to emotion scores (e.g. Eerola & Anderson 2026 meta-analysis). Designs that shift representation through repeated internal rehearsal and then measure the resulting change in affective prediction are, at present, almost nowhere to be found.

**Current academic consensus and related literature**  
In machine learning more broadly, concepts such as experience replay, dream-like replay, and memory consolidation via offline replay exist (especially in reinforcement learning and brain-inspired models). These, however, are aimed primarily at improving learning efficiency or consolidating memory, not at observing drift for the sake of changes in affective prediction. In the music-emotion context, this direction is currently scarce.

**Confirmed nuances and points of contention**  
The term “dream training” is used as a metaphor for offline replay / systems consolidation in neuroscience, but direct identification with human INMI is not well supported. Machine implementations would need separate validation of correspondence to the human phenomenon.

**Present status and open issues**  
The proposal stands as an original question beyond the existing MER framework. Feasibility and correspondence to the human phenomenon remain future research tasks.

## Stage 3 — Function of the Mechanism

**Core of your understanding**  
Under current theory the function is not yet settled. Hypotheses include memory consolidation, mood regulation, and epiphenomenon. On your view, the drift R → R\* is not “correction of error” but “re-processing of the value and felt quality of the piece for the present self.”

**Cross-check with primary / major sources**  
Liikkanen & Jakubowski (2020) explicitly state that the possible function of INMI is not yet fully understood. Memory-consolidation and mood-regulation hypotheses are suggested but lack decisive evidence.

**Current academic consensus and related literature**  
- Memory consolidation: suggested by Floridou et al., Liikkanen, and others; repetitive INMI may contribute to imprinting new musical memories.  
- Mood regulation: affective evaluation of INMI (pleasant / unpleasant) is known to be person- and situation-dependent.  
- Epiphenomenon: a side-effect of how auditory memory and the phonological loop operate remains a viable view.

**Confirmed nuances and points of contention**  
Interpreting the process as “re-processing” is consistent with both consolidation and mood-regulation hypotheses. Formalizing it as “re-processing of value and felt quality,” however, is an original hypothesis without direct empirical support at present.

**Present status and open issues**  
The assessment that the function remains unresolved is accurate, as is the placement of your hypothesis. The “re-processing” hypothesis is a high-potential target for future empirical verification.

## Stage 4 — Field Placement

**Core of your understanding**  
The intersection of music psychology (Musical Imagery × Music & Emotion) and research on spontaneous cognition / involuntary memory. If the machine side is pursued, it also enters computational music cognition. The distinctive element is not merely observing human INMI, but deliberately inducing R → R\* drift inside a machine and examining the resulting change in affective prediction.

**Cross-check and evaluation**  
This placement is consistent with existing field divisions. INMI research already sits at the intersection of music psychology and spontaneous cognition; making affective output (change in emotion scores) explicit strengthens the link to Music & Emotion. The machine side constitutes a new question within computational music cognition.

**Present status and open issues**  
The placement is appropriate. The claim of originality (inducing drift inside a machine) is supported as an element not found in the existing literature.

## Open Questions and Overall Status

The open questions listed (dimensions of drift, conditions for amplification vs. correction, machine architecture) are all academically productive unresolved problems.

**Overall status and open issues**  
- The empirical base for the INMI phenomenon itself is solid.  
- Formalizing the process as R → R\*, and the hypothesis that it modulates affective responses, currently lack direct empirical support and stand as original hypotheses.  
- The distinction between knowledge learning and music learning (presence/absence of ground truth; affective fluctuation as observable outcome) is consistent with related fields and stands as an original formalization.  
- Inducing drift inside a machine is a new question beyond the existing MER framework.

### Remaining Questions and Research Tasks to Pursue

1. **Empirical measurement of R → R\* drift**  
   Measure, in human experiments, which dimensions (valence, arousal, semantic content, structural expectation) drift most strongly through internal replay.

2. **Modulation of affective response on re-listening**  
   How accumulated R\* changes valence / arousal scores when the same piece is heard again.

3. **Conditions for switching between amplification and correction**  
   Under what conditions (novelty of the piece, mood at listening, number of repetitions) internal replay functions as amplification versus correction.

4. **Design of machine architectures**  
   Implementation methods for internal rehearsal that produce drift patterns close to the human phenomenon.

5. **Theoretical and empirical development of the “re-processing” hypothesis**  
   Formalize R → R\* as re-processing of value and felt quality, and clarify its relation to consolidation and mood-regulation hypotheses.
