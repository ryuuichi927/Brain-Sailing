# Record of the Examination of Musical Ethos

This document reconstructs a series of reflections that began with the ancient Greek theory of musical ethos and proceeded through the divergence between ancient and modern listening, historical turning points, the origins of authority, underlying logic, developments involving Nietzsche, Wagner and Beethoven, and cross-cultural comparisons. Priority is given to preserving the trajectory of thought rather than presenting only polished final conclusions. The movement from claim → problem → revision is made visible.

## Stage 0 — Starting Point: Introduction of the Concept of Ethos

**Initial Claim:** In ancient Greece, music was held to possess an objective “ethos” (character / ethical disposition) that could imitate (mimesis), habituate, and form the soul of the listener.  
**Emerging Problem:** How does this premise continue or rupture in relation to modern listening experience?  
**Revision / Settlement:** The Pythagorean idea of cosmic–soul harmony and the lineage from Damon to Plato were confirmed, positioning ethos theory as the claim that music exerts an objective formative power on the soul.

The core of musical ethos theory is the premise that music is not merely entertainment or expression, but an educational and ethical apparatus that actively shapes the listener’s character. In the *Republic*, Plato carefully selected modes for the education of the guardians, recommending the Dorian for courage and steadfastness and the Phrygian for moderation, while excluding modes associated with softness and lamentation. Aristotle allowed greater flexibility through catharsis, yet agreed that music affects the character of the soul. At this stage, ethos is treated as something residing in the music, and the listener occupies the position of the one who receives it.

## Stage 1 — Initial Intuition: The Divergence between Ancient and Modern

**Initial Claim:** In antiquity the listener is unilaterally influenced by the ethos residing in the music (passive); in modernity, even the same piece allows the listener to “replace” or “apply” its ethos according to personal experience and identity (active).  
**Emerging Problem:** Validity of the hypothesis that the trigger remains common, yet the subject reverses at the moment influence begins.  
**Revision / Settlement:** Modern individualization was provisionally settled as turning on the “discovery of selfhood.”

In the ancient model, the ethos of the music holds the initiative and shapes the listener’s soul. In modernity, the same musical work acquires different meanings for different listeners, and the listener side reinterprets and rearranges the ethos. This reversal is supported by the loss of a shared sacred order and by the modern view of identity as a project assembled by the self rather than something conferred from outside.

## Stage 2 — Discovery and Correction of Causal Weakness

**Initial Claim:** “The moment the shared harmony of the gods or the cosmos disappeared, music changed from something to be received into material to be used.”  
**Emerging Problem:** Self-critique that this causal account is too abrupt. Loss of sacred order alone does not sufficiently explain the transformation into “material.”  
**Revision / Settlement:** The loss of order merely removed the reason for receiving; the modern conception of identity as something the self assembles filled the resulting vacancy. The causal chain was reconstructed in two stages.

> 🔁 Revision:  
> The single-line causality “loss of sacred order → music becomes material” is insufficient. The loss only eliminated the ground for receiving; what filled the vacancy was the modern conception of identity as a project the self constructs. The causality was reorganized into two stages.

This revision reset the question “Why do modern people not simply receive ethos but instead personalize, digest, and replace it?” under more precise historical and intellectual conditions.

## Stage 3 — Dual Layering of Historical Turning Points

**Initial Claim:** When did “personal music” come into being?  
**Emerging Problem:** The complexity that cannot be reduced to a single turning point (Industrial Revolution, wars, democratization, etc.).  
**Revision / Settlement:** Divided into two layers—intellectual (Romanticism) and technological/practical (phonograph to Walkman).

The intellectual turning point is Romanticism (late 18th to early 19th century). Here music shifted from something that imitates cosmic harmony or shared affect to something that expresses the individual’s inner life, genius, and subjectivity. On the technological and practical side, the phonograph (late 19th century) detached music from the site of performance, and the Walkman (1979) made music a portable personal soundtrack. Consequently, the history of “personal music” is at most 200–300 years long—an extremely brief exception within the tens of thousands of years of human musical history.

## Stage 4 — Tracing the Origins of the Authority of Ethos

**Initial Claim:** On what did the “absoluteness” of ethos rest before Romanticism?  
**Emerging Problem:** Can it be reduced to sacredness alone, or did experiential efficacy come first?  
**Revision / Settlement:** Explained by two pillars—the numerical correspondence with cosmic/divine order, and the long-accumulated experiential effect (“it works”). Sequence: experience first, theory later granting authentication.

In the tradition from the Pythagoreans to Plato, the numerical ratios of music were held to be identical with the structure of the cosmos and of the soul. This correspondence provided the theoretical foundation of absoluteness. At the same time, the repeated experience that music alters emotion, synchronizes groups, constitutes ritual, and sometimes acts therapeutically underpinned its authority. Ethos was not first a theory; experience preceded, and theory later authenticated it in Greece.

## Stage 5 — Digging into the Underlying Logic (Original Hypothesis)

**Initial Claim:** Human desire for explanation (curiosity) is the starting point; “god” is a result of that desire, not its origin.  
**Emerging Problem:** Positioning of the hypothesis that pleasure amplifies when explanations of external-world change and of inner change become structurally linked.  
**Revision / Settlement:** (Hypothesis) A model was proposed in which the linkage of the two explanations multiplies pleasure. Ben countered with Huron’s theory of expectation and entrainment (bodily synchronization), leaving the priority of the foundational layer unresolved.

(Hypothesis) Humans observe change, experience corresponding inner change, and seek explanations for both. Satisfaction of the explanatory desire activates the reward system; when the two explanations become structurally linked (e.g., melodic climax × narrative × reflection of worldly change such as thunder or volcanic eruption), pleasure amplifies multiplicatively rather than additively.

Ben suggested that this hypothesis may fit better as an explanation of “why humans feel compelled to invent theories such as ethos theory” than as an explanation of “why music moves people.” Entrainment, moreover, occurs even in non-linguistic organisms and is therefore older and more foundational than the desire for explanation. The question of which layer—“interpretive linkage” or “bodily synchronization”—is foundational remained open.

## Stage 6 — Expansion to Nietzsche and Wagner, and Self-Correction

**Initial Claim:** Nietzsche’s Dionysian in *The Birth of Tragedy* (breaking the wall of individuation and returning to the primal One) and the ideal of the composer as a “self-effacing conduit.” Reading Wagner’s total work of art as its embodiment.  
**Emerging Problem:** Whether Beethoven and Wagner can be equated, and the actual relation between Nietzsche’s theory and Wagner.  
**Revision / Settlement:** The user himself pushed back against Ben’s framing and repositioned both as “authors who design ethos.”

> 🔁 Revision:  
> Beethoven and Wagner are essentially the same: both design ethos themselves. Wagner’s thrust merely happened to coincide with Nietzsche’s theory. Nietzsche’s Dionysian theory is an entirely different model that surrenders self-control; Nietzsche may have misrecognized Wagner as the embodiment of his own theory. Ben accepted this and reset the axis to “ethos designed by the author” versus “ethos as self-dissolving conduction.”

This self-correction clarified the question of power—“who designs and owns ethos?”—as the central axis of subsequent inquiry.

## Stage 7 — Connection to Beethoven

**Initial Claim:** Applying the perspective of “a gekokujō (upward usurpation) over the right to control ethos” to Beethoven.  
**Emerging Problem:** Why the conditions for a composer to design and own ethos personally became available in Beethoven’s era.  
**Revision / Settlement:** The collapse of the patronage system and the emergence of the concert market created those conditions. After the double rupture of disappointment with Napoleon and the Heiligenstadt Testament, the Ninth Symphony can be read as an attempt to re-forge ethos with one’s own hands.

Beethoven occupies a symbolic position of transition in which the composer becomes the subject who designs ethos without depending on traditional sacred order or the authority of patrons. The Ninth Symphony, as its culmination, bears the character of a personal re-forging of ethos.

## Stage 8 — The “Ideological Time Bomb” Hypothesis

**Initial Claim:** Historically the Ninth has repeatedly “detonated” at moments when individual self-pursuit reached a critical point.  
**Emerging Problem:** Is ethos a constantly operating attribute, or something understood only when certain conditions are met?  
**Revision / Settlement:** (Hypothesis) Ethos is a time-device that is “understood” only when specific historical and psychological conditions align.

(Hypothesis) Bernstein’s 1989 “Ode to Freedom” performance after the fall of the Berlin Wall, its adoption as the EU anthem, and the Japanese year-end custom of performing the Ninth (rooted in the postwar recovery period) illustrate how the Ninth has repeatedly detonated at junctures of intense individual self-pursuit or communal redefinition. Ethos may function not as a constantly active attribute but as a time-device activated only under particular conditions.

## Stage 9 — Expansion into Cross-Cultural Comparison

**Initial Claim:** Comparing the Western single-line model (“disappearance of sacred order → authorial usurpation → listener individualization”) with other cultures.  
**Emerging Problem:** The premise “individualization = product of modernity” is shaken by cross-cultural cases.  
**Revision / Settlement:** A direction emerged for reconstructing the single-line historical model along three axes: locus of attachment, authority to design, and passive–active relation.

### China: Confucian Theory of Ritual and Music

In the “harmony of heaven and earth” thought of the *Record of Music* (*Yueji*), music corresponds to the principle of central harmony between heaven and earth; “music is that which communicates with ethics.” When the sage-king establishes music, he is transmitting heavenly principle; authorial design and transmission of heavenly principle coincide from the outset. The Western drama of “usurpation from the gods” simply does not arise.

### West Africa: The Griot

Ethos is not an abstract modal theory but adheres to the hereditary specialist stratum (the griot) as person and lineage. Praise songs are custom-made for specific rulers, families, or historical events and are embedded in relationships. The model is not one in which the listener actively replaces meaning; the specialist designs and the recipient receives.

### Northern Europe: The Sámi Joik

One does not sing “about” a person but “joiks” the person—directly invoking or embodying them. At birth each Sámi is given a unique joik that is extremely personal yet simultaneously gift-like and relational. Extreme individualization existed for thousands of years without waiting for Romanticism, strongly challenging the premise that individualization is a modern product. Here the power of individualization lies with the giver (parent or elder), not the receiver.

These three cases demonstrate the necessity of grasping “who controls ethos” not as a single historical line but along at least three independent axes (locus of attachment / authority to design / passive–active).

## Open Questions

1. **Priority of Interpretive Linkage vs. Bodily Synchronization**  
   The issue left open in Stage 5. Positioning entrainment (bodily and affective synchronization) as the evolutionarily older foundational layer and “reward amplification through explanatory linkage” as a later cognitive superstructure is promising, yet final prioritization remains incomplete.

2. **Model Reconstruction via the Three Cross-Cultural Axes**  
   How to integrate the three axes (locus of attachment / authority to design / passive–active) extracted from the Chinese, Griot, and Sámi comparisons into the original Western-centric single-line historical model. In particular, how to theorize the Sámi joik’s “extremely personal yet gift-like” model as distinct from modern individualization.

3. **Generalization of the Temporality of Ethos**  
   Whether the “time-bomb” hypothesis of Stage 8 can be extended to works and cultures beyond the Ninth. How far the view that ethos is not a constantly operating attribute but a device activated under historical and psychological conditions can be taken.

4. **Connection to the Episode Model**  
   How concretely to connect the underlying logic (desire for explanation and reward, linkage of worldly and inner change) to the theory of the Episode (meaningful listening experience) in music psychology. Integration with Huron’s theory of expectation and conversion into an empirically testable form remain open.
